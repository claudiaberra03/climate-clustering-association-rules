# Clustering and Association Rules for Local Climate Change Analysis with LARS-WG

This bachelor thesis studies whether two data-mining techniques, k-means clustering and association rules, can extract local climate-change patterns from observed and synthetic daily temperature series. Observed data for 28 weather stations in the provinces of Milan, Como and Varese (Italy) are compared with synthetic series generated by the stochastic weather generator LARS-WG 8.0 for 2035 and 2055 under two emission scenarios.

The thesis (in Italian) was written for the BSc in Mathematical Engineering at Politecnico di Milano, academic year 2024/25, under the supervision of Prof. Alessandro Campi.

**Authors:** Claudia Berra, Gaia Di Paolo.

## Question and data

The question is to what extent local trends of climate change can be identified from daily minimum and maximum temperatures. Three components are used:

- **Observations.** Daily minimum and maximum temperature and daily precipitation from ARPA Lombardia, for the 28 stations with at least about 20 years of records. The year 2020 is taken as representative of the current climate.
- **Synthetic series.** LARS-WG 8.0 is calibrated on the observed records and generates daily weather for 2031–2070. It applies the changes projected by the global climate model CMCC-ESM2 (CMIP6) under the scenarios SSP1-2.6 (sustainable development) and SSP5-8.5 (fossil-fuelled development).
- **Analysed datasets.** For each station: the 2020 observations, and the synthetic years 2035 and 2055 under each scenario. The nine days of 2020 with invalid measurements are excluded; the association rules use the same 356 days in every year.

## Method

**Clustering (chapter 3).**
- Each station is described by its daily minimum and maximum temperatures over the year. The temperatures are standardised (z-score) and the k-means algorithm is applied with scikit-learn.
- The number of clusters (k = 3) is chosen with the elbow method on the 2020 data and kept fixed for all years. The number of restarts is chosen from the inertia as a function of `n_init`.
- To compare two partitions, the labels are first aligned with the permutation that maximises their agreement (Hungarian algorithm on the confusion matrix). The agreement is then measured as the fraction of stations with the same label, together with the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI).
- The whole analysis is repeated without standardisation.

**Association rules (chapter 4).**
- Temperatures are mapped to categories, from "extremely cold" to "extremely hot".
- Each station-day becomes a transaction with four items: the minimum and maximum category in 2020 and on the same day of the future year.
- Frequent itemsets are extracted with Apriori (mlxtend). Only rules of the form "2020 category → future category" are kept. They are evaluated with support, confidence, lift, leverage, conviction and Zhang's metric.
- The behaviour of these metrics is studied as the categorisation becomes finer (five levels of granularity).

## Results

Agreement between clusterings (accuracy after label alignment / ARI), computed from the saved partitions by `scripts/compare_clusterings.py`:

| Comparison | Standardised | Not standardised |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2020 vs 2035 or 2055, either scenario | 53.6% / 0.18–0.19 | 75.0% / 0.35 |
| 2035 vs 2055, SSP1-2.6 | 100% / 1 | 100% / 1 |
| 2035 vs 2055, SSP5-8.5 | 96.4% / 0.87 | 100% / 1 |
| SSP1-2.6 vs SSP5-8.5, 2035 | 100% / 1 | 100% / 1 |
| SSP1-2.6 vs SSP5-8.5, 2055 | 96.4% / 0.87 | 100% / 1 |

**Clustering findings.**
- Between 2020 and the projected years only about half of the stations keep their group. Similar climates today do not imply similar future trajectories, even in a small region.
- Between 2035 and 2055 the groups are essentially stable; the only change appears under SSP5-8.5.
- The two scenarios give almost identical partitions in the same year. The stability also reflects the fact that a single global model applies spatially coherent changes over a small area.
- Standardisation matters: without it, the maximum temperatures dominate and the partitions look more stable over time.

**Association-rule findings.**
- With minimum support 0.01 and confidence 0.3 at the intermediate granularity, between 63 and 66 rules are kept per scenario. The most frequent rules link moderate categories to the same categories (for example, a mild minimum in 2020 to a mild minimum in 2035, support 0.30, confidence 0.76, lift 1.92), so they do not show clear changes.
- Lowering the thresholds reveals less frequent rules that involve more extreme categories. Warming rules gain weight over time, and some reach a lift above 3. They clearly prevail in 2055 under SSP5-8.5. Cooling rules have a lift close to 1.
- As the categorisation becomes finer, support, confidence and leverage decrease. The lift peaks at the fourth level of granularity. These trends are the same for every year and scenario.

**A correction to the thesis.** In Table 3.4 of the thesis (and the corresponding presentation slide), the 2055 SSP1-2.6 vs SSP5-8.5 row reports 100% for the standardised data and 96.43% for the non-standardised data. The saved partitions give the opposite: 96.43% (ARI 0.87) standardised and 100% non-standardised. This is the only combination consistent with the other rows of Tables 3.3 and 3.4, so the two columns of that row appear to be swapped. The thesis also rounds the ARI of the 2020 vs 2055 SSP5-8.5 comparison to 0.18, while the saved partitions give 0.190.

## Limitations

- **A single generator, model and year.** LARS-WG uses statistical downscaling, which tends to underrepresent extreme events. All projections come from a single global model, CMCC-ESM2. Each period is represented by one year, so year-to-year variability is not averaged out.
- **Only temperatures are clustered.** Precipitation, humidity and solar radiation are not included in the clustering, and solar radiation was not available for the generator either.
- **No predictive meaning.** A stochastic weather generator does not forecast the weather of a specific year. It produces synthetic series that are statistically similar to the observations, with the projected changes applied.
- **Annual transactions.** The association rules mix all seasons in the same transactions. A seasonal analysis may give more homogeneous patterns.

## Reproduce the analyses

The input datasets are not distributed (see [data/README.md](data/README.md)). The saved outputs are included, so the comparison of the clusterings can be reproduced without them:

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt
python scripts/compare_clusterings.py      # writes results/cluster_comparison.csv
```

With the input files in place, run the notebooks from their own folders:

- **Clustering.** In `clustering/`, run `clustering_2020.ipynb`, then `clustering_synthetic.ipynb` once per year and scenario (change the file names in the first cells), then `compare_clusterings.ipynb`. The maps use `geopandas` and `contextily`, which download the base map tiles.
- **Association rules.** In `association_rules/`, run one notebook per year and scenario. They write the frequent itemsets, the rules and the metric plots to `association_rules/output/`.

The analysis code was rerun with Python 3.13, pandas 3.0.6, scikit-learn 1.9.1 and mlxtend 0.25.0. It reproduced the saved 2020 partition and the number of rules for the scenarios that were checked (66 rules for 2035 SSP1-2.6 and 63 for 2055 SSP5-8.5).
